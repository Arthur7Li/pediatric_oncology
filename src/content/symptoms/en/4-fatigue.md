# Managing Cancer-Related Fatigue & Anemia

Seeing your child utterly exhausted is one of the most difficult parts of cancer treatment. Cancer-related fatigue is not like normal tiredness; it is a profound, whole-body exhaustion that doesn't always get better with sleep.

Often, this fatigue is directly tied to **anemia**—a condition where the body does not have enough red blood cells to carry oxygen to its tissues.

## Understanding Anemia

Red blood cells are produced in the bone marrow and act as the body's oxygen delivery system. Chemotherapy and radiation can temporarily damage the bone marrow, causing a sharp drop in red blood cell production.

- **Hemoglobin:** This is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Your oncology team will closely monitor your child's hemoglobin levels through regular Complete Blood Counts (CBC).

### Transfusion Thresholds in Canada

In Canadian pediatric oncology centers (following guidelines from groups like POGO and the C17 Council), blood transfusions are carefully managed to provide relief while minimizing risks:

- Generally, a red blood cell transfusion is considered if a child's hemoglobin drops below **70 g/L (7.0 g/dL)**.
- However, the decision is not based on numbers alone. Doctors will assess your child's symptoms. If your child is extremely symptomatic (severe headaches, dizzy, or breathless) at a slightly higher number, a transfusion may still be ordered.
- A blood transfusion can act like "liquid energy," often providing noticeable relief within 24 hours.

## Home Management & Energy Conservation

While waiting for blood counts to recover, you can help manage your child's energy:

- **Pacing:** Treat energy like a daily allowance. Break activities into short 10-15 minute intervals, followed by rest.
- **Prioritize:** Let your child choose the one or two activities they most want to do that day, and skip the rest.
- **School Accommodations:** Work with your hospital's school liaison or social worker. Your child may need a modified schedule (e.g., half-days), a quiet place to rest, or permission to skip physical education classes.
- **Nutrition:** While iron-rich foods (like lean meats, beans, and spinach) are healthy, **diet alone cannot fix chemotherapy-induced anemia**. Focus on offering small, frequent, appealing meals rather than stressing over specific nutrients.

## 🚨 When to Call the Care Team (Red Flags)

Fatigue is expected, but certain symptoms indicate that anemia has reached a critical, potentially dangerous level. **Seek immediate medical attention if your child experiences:**

- **Shortness of breath** or difficulty breathing, even when resting.
- **Dizziness, lightheadedness**, or fainting (especially when standing up).
- **A rapid or pounding heartbeat** (palpitations) while at rest.
- **Extremely pale skin**, or pale/white gums and nail beds.
- Any sudden, severe change in their level of alertness or ability to wake up.

> **Note:** If fatigue is accompanied by a fever (38.0°C / 100.4°F or higher), treat it as a neutropenic fever emergency and go to the ER immediately.
