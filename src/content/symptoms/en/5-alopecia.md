Hair loss, medically known as alopecia, is one of the most visible and emotionally distressing side effects of cancer treatment. While it does not cause physical harm, it fundamentally changes how a child looks and, often, how they feel about themselves.

It is important to remember—and to remind your child—that hair loss is temporary. It is a sign that the medicine is working hard to fight the fast-growing cancer cells.

## What to Expect (The Timeline)

Not all chemotherapy drugs cause hair loss, and radiation only causes hair loss in the specific area being treated. If your child's protocol includes hair-loss-inducing drugs, here is what typically happens:

- **Weeks 2 to 3:** Hair usually begins to thin or fall out roughly two to three weeks after the first chemotherapy session.
- **Scalp Sensitivity:** Just before the hair falls out, your child may complain that their scalp feels tender, itchy, or overly sensitive.
- **Clumping:** Hair may fall out gradually or come out in large clumps on their pillow or in the shower.
- **Body Hair:** Loss of eyebrows, eyelashes, and body hair may also occur, though sometimes later than scalp hair.

## Emotional Support

For many children and teens, losing their hair is when the illness becomes "real" to the outside world.

- **Frame it as bravery:** Remind them that their bald head is a sign of their strength and the medicine working.
- **Give them control:** Many families choose to cut the child's hair short or shave it before it starts falling out in clumps. Let your child guide this decision. Some make it a family event, with parents or siblings shaving their heads in solidarity.
- **Validate feelings:** It is entirely normal for your child to feel angry, sad, or self-conscious. Listen without immediately trying to "fix" it.

## Scalp Care

A bald scalp requires gentle care:

- **Sun Protection:** The scalp is highly vulnerable to sunburn. Always use a high-SPF, broad-spectrum sunscreen and a hat when outdoors.
- **Gentle Washing:** Use a mild baby shampoo and pat the head dry with a soft towel. Avoid scrubbing.
- **Warmth:** Without hair, children lose body heat quickly. Keep soft cotton caps handy, especially at night or in air-conditioned hospitals.

## Wigs and Head Coverings in Canada

Your child may choose to wear hats, soft beanies, bandanas, or a wig—or they may prefer to rock their bald head.

If your child wants a wig, there are exceptional Canadian programs that provide high-quality hair prosthetics to pediatric patients free of charge:

- **Angel Hair for Kids (A Child's Voice Foundation):** A national program providing custom wigs and hair loss solutions to financially disadvantaged children across Canada.
- **Wigs for Kids BC:** A dedicated program providing free wigs to children in British Columbia.
- **Your Hospital Social Worker:** Your oncology social worker at centers like SickKids or CHU Sainte-Justine can connect you with local "wig banks" or specific provincial programs.

## Regrowth: What Happens Next?

Hair will begin to grow back after the chemotherapy that causes alopecia is completed, though it may take several weeks to see a shadow of growth.

- **"Chemo Curls":** When hair grows back, it often has a completely different texture or color. It may be darker, lighter, or grow back incredibly curly—a phenomenon affectionately known as "chemo curls." This texture change is usually temporary, and the hair will eventually return to its original state.
