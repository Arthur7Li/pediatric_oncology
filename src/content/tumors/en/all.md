Receiving a diagnosis of Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) is terrifying for any family. However, pediatric ALL is one of the greatest success stories in modern medicine: over the past five decades, collaborative clinical research in Canada and internationally has elevated five-year survival rates from under 10% to greater than 90%.

This guide is designed to explain what ALL is, how Canadian medical teams classify risk, and what to expect during each phase of treatment in compassionate, accessible terms.

---

## What is Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia?

Leukemia is a cancer of the blood and bone marrow—the spongy tissue inside bones where blood cells are made.

- In healthy bone marrow, stem cells develop into mature red blood cells (which carry oxygen), white blood cells (which fight infections), and platelets (which stop bleeding).
- In ALL, the bone marrow produces too many immature white blood cells called **lymphoblasts** (or "blasts"). These abnormal cells cannot fight infection, and they multiply rapidly, crowding out healthy blood cells.

### Immunophenotypes: B-Cell vs. T-Cell ALL

During diagnostic workups at pediatric centers (such as SickKids, CHU Sainte-Justine, or BC Children's), flow cytometry will determine the exact subtype:

- **B-Cell ALL (B-ALL):** Accounts for approximately 80–85% of pediatric cases. Blasts originate from B-lymphocyte precursors.
- **T-Cell ALL (T-ALL):** Accounts for approximately 15% of cases. Blasts originate from T-lymphocyte precursors and often present with higher initial white blood cell counts or a mass in the chest (mediastinum).

## Common Symptoms

Symptoms of ALL often develop over a few days or weeks as leukemic blasts replace normal blood cells:

- **Anemia (Low Red Blood Cells):** Fatigue, pale skin, weakness, cold sensitivity, and shortness of breath.
- **Thrombocytopenia (Low Platelets):** Easy bruising, petechiae (tiny red/purple pinpoint spots on the skin), frequent nosebleeds, or bleeding gums.
- **Neutropenia (Low Infection-Fighting White Cells):** Recurrent fevers and frequent infections that do not resolve.
- **Bone and Joint Pain:** A dull or sharp ache caused by the crowding of blasts expanding within the bone marrow, sometimes causing a limp.
- **Organ Enlargement:** Swollen lymph nodes (neck, underarms, groin) or a swollen, tender belly due to an enlarged spleen or liver.

## Risk Stratification: Tailoring Treatment to Your Child

Canadian pediatric oncology centers participate in **C17 Council** and **Children's Oncology Group (COG)** clinical protocols. Rather than giving every child the same heavy regimen, oncologists categorize ALL into risk groups:

1. **Standard Risk (SR):** Children aged 1 to 9.99 years with an initial white blood cell (WBC) count under 50,000/µL and favorable cytogenetic markers.
2. **High Risk (HR):** Children aged 10 and older, or children with initial WBC counts of 50,000/µL or higher.
3. **Very High Risk / Infant ALL:** Specific genetic rearrangements (e.g., _KMT2A_ / _MLL_ rearrangements in infants under 1 year, or Philadelphia chromosome-positive _BCR-ABL1_).

### Measurable Residual Disease (MRD)

At day 8/15 of induction and at the end of the first month, doctors perform a bone marrow aspiration to measure **Minimal (Measurable) Residual Disease (MRD)**. MRD detects whether even 1 leukemic cell remains among 10,000 to 100,000 normal cells. Children who achieve early MRD negativity have the highest cure rates and may avoid unnecessarily intensive medications.

## The Treatment Roadmap

Treatment for ALL typically lasts between **2 and 2.5 years** and is delivered in distinct, structured phases:

### 1. Induction (Month 1)

- **Goal:** Destroy the vast majority of leukemic cells and achieve complete remission (less than 5% blasts in bone marrow and return of normal blood counts).
- **Core Medications:** Dexamethasone/Prednisone, Vincristine, PEG-Asparaginase (or Erwinia asparaginase), and Daunorubicin (for high-risk).
- **CNS Prophylaxis:** Intrathecal chemotherapy (methotrexate injected into the spinal fluid via lumbar puncture) to protect the central nervous system.

### 2. Consolidation / Intensification (Months 2–8)

- **Goal:** Eliminate any remaining hidden blasts that could cause a relapse.
- **Core Medications:** High-dose Methotrexate, Mercaptopurine (6-MP), Cyclophosphamide, Cytarabine, and ongoing lumbar punctures.

### 3. Interim Maintenance & Delayed Intensification

- Cycles designed to reinforce remission while allowing bone marrow recovery before entering the final phase.

### 4. Maintenance Therapy (Remaining ~1.5 to 2 Years)

- **Goal:** Prevent recurrence through steady, lower-intensity oral medications (daily 6-MP and weekly oral methotrexate), with periodic pulses of steroid and vincristine.
- Most children attend school, resume friendships, and participate in normal childhood activities during maintenance while receiving regular blood count monitoring.

## Targeted Therapies & Advances in Canada

For children with high-risk genetic variations or refractory disease, Canadian centers have adopted cutting-edge targeted treatments:

- **Immunotherapy (Blinatumomab):** A bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) antibody that connects the child's own immune cells to CD19 on leukemic blasts, proving highly effective with lower toxicity than traditional chemotherapy.
- **CAR-T Cell Therapy (Kymriah / Tisagenlecleucel):** Genetically reprogramming a child's own T-cells to seek and destroy refractory B-ALL, available at leading pediatric quaternary centers across Canada.

## Supporting Your Child at Home

- **Infection Prevention:** When absolute neutrophil counts (ANC) are low, practice strict hand hygiene, avoid crowded spaces, and seek immediate emergency care for any fever of 38.0°C (100.4°F) or higher.
- **Nutrition & Hydration:** High-dose steroids can dramatically increase appetite and cause mood swings; maintain gentle boundaries and focus on nutrient-dense foods.
- **Emotional Support:** Connect with the hospital's Child Life Specialists and social workers to help your child and their siblings process the treatment journey.
