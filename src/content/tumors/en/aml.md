Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) is a fast-growing cancer of the blood and bone marrow. While less common than Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL), it requires a distinct and very intense approach to treatment. 

In Canada, pediatric oncology centers work closely together through the **C17 Council** to ensure that children with AML receive the most advanced and effective treatments available.

---

## What is Acute Myeloid Leukemia?

In healthy bone marrow, stem cells develop into mature blood cells. In AML, the bone marrow produces too many abnormal, immature white blood cells called **myeloblasts**. 

These leukemic cells quickly crowd out healthy, functioning blood cells, leading to a shortage of:
- **Red blood cells:** causing anemia.
- **Platelets:** causing bleeding and bruising.
- **Normal white blood cells:** leaving the body vulnerable to serious infections.

### How is AML Different from ALL?

While both are leukemias, they originate from different types of cells. AML tends to progress more quickly and requires much more intensive, condensed cycles of chemotherapy. Unlike ALL, which involves years of maintenance therapy, AML treatment is usually completed in about 6 months, though these months are spent almost entirely in the hospital.

## Common Symptoms

Symptoms of AML often appear quickly and mimic common childhood illnesses, but they do not improve with standard care:
- **Fatigue and Weakness:** Due to low red blood cells (anemia).
- **Easy Bruising and Bleeding:** Frequent nosebleeds, bleeding gums, or tiny red spots on the skin (petechiae) due to low platelets.
- **Fever and Frequent Infections:** Due to a lack of healthy, infection-fighting white blood cells.
- **Bone or Joint Pain:** Caused by the buildup of leukemic cells in the marrow.
- **Swollen Gums or Skin Lumps:** Sometimes, leukemic cells can build up in the gums or form small lumps under the skin (chloromas).

## The Treatment Roadmap

Treatment for AML in Canada is generally divided into two main phases and is characterized by very intensive chemotherapy regimens. 

### 1. Induction Therapy

- **Goal:** To destroy as many leukemic cells as possible and achieve a complete remission.
- **Process:** This phase usually involves two intense cycles of chemotherapy (often using medications like Cytarabine and Daunorubicin).
- **Hospitalization:** Because the treatment severely suppresses the bone marrow, children usually remain in the hospital for 3-4 weeks per cycle to manage severe neutropenia (high risk of infection) and need frequent blood transfusions.

### 2. Consolidation Therapy (Post-Remission)

- **Goal:** To eliminate any hidden, remaining leukemic cells (Minimal Residual Disease or MRD) and prevent a relapse.
- **Process:** Depending on the genetic markers of the leukemia and the child's response to induction, this phase involves either:
  - Additional cycles of intensive chemotherapy.
  - A **Stem Cell Transplant (Bone Marrow Transplant)**. This is often recommended for high-risk AML or if the leukemia does not respond well to initial chemotherapy. Canadian quaternary centers (like SickKids, CHU Sainte-Justine, and BC Children's) specialize in these complex procedures.

## Supporting Your Child at Home and in Hospital

- **Infection Prevention is Critical:** The profound neutropenia associated with AML treatment makes your child highly susceptible to life-threatening infections. Strict handwashing, isolation protocols, and immediate action for any fever (above 38.0°C / 100.4°F) are absolutely necessary.
- **Nutritional Support:** Intensive chemotherapy often causes severe nausea, mouth sores (mucositis), and loss of appetite. Your child may need a feeding tube or intravenous nutrition (TPN) to maintain their strength.
- **Blood Transfusions:** Expect your child to need numerous red blood cell and platelet transfusions throughout their treatment.

---

## Looking Forward

The journey through AML is undeniably rigorous and physically demanding. However, advancements in genetic profiling, targeted therapies, and supportive care across Canadian pediatric centers continue to improve outcomes and survival rates for children facing this diagnosis.
