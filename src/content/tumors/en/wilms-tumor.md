Discovering a lump in your young child's abdomen is an alarming experience. Wilms tumor (also known as nephroblastoma) is the most common kidney cancer affecting children. Thanks to decades of clinical trials and multi-institutional cooperation, Wilms tumor has an overall cure rate exceeding 90% for most children.

This guide explains the biology, histology, staging, and treatment pathways for Wilms tumor within the Canadian healthcare system.

---

## What is Wilms Tumor?

Wilms tumor arises from immature kidney cells (metanephric blastema) that were meant to develop into normal renal tissue during fetal life. Instead, these precursor cells persist and begin growing abnormally into a solid mass within the kidney.

- **Peak Age:** Most commonly diagnosed in toddlers and young children between the ages of 2 and 5 years (rare after age 8).
- **Unilateral vs. Bilateral:** In about 90–95% of cases, the tumor develops in only one kidney (unilateral). In 5–10% of cases, both kidneys are affected simultaneously or sequentially (bilateral, Stage V).

---

## Warning: Gentle Handling of the Abdomen

> [!CAUTION]
> **Important Safety Note for Caregivers & Clinicians:**
> If a Wilms tumor is suspected, **do not press, palpate, or massage your child's abdomen firmly**. Wilms tumors are encased in a thin capsule that can rupture if pressed hard, which can spill cancer cells into the peritoneal cavity and elevate the cancer's stage.

## Common Signs and Symptoms

Wilms tumors often grow quietly and may become quite large before causing obvious illness:

- **Painless Abdominal Mass or Swelling:** Frequently noticed by a parent when bathing, changing diapers, or carrying the child.
- **Hematuria (Blood in Urine):** Urine may appear pink, cola-colored, or red in about 20–30% of children.
- **Hypertension (High Blood Pressure):** The tumor can compress renal blood vessels, triggering the release of renin and elevating blood pressure.
- **Low-Grade Fever or Malaise:** General fussiness or decreased energy.
- **Abdominal Discomfort:** Mild aching or nausea as the mass pushes against other abdominal organs.

---

## Histology: Favorable vs. Anaplastic

When the tumor tissue is examined under a microscope, pathologists categorize it into two distinct histological categories:

1. **Favorable Histology (FH):** Seen in approximately 90% of children. The cells resemble normal developing embryonic kidney structures and respond exceptionally well to standard chemotherapy and surgery. Cure rates exceed 90–95%.
2. **Anaplastic (Unfavorable) Histology:** Seen in about 10% of cases. The cell nuclei appear enlarged, hyperchromatic, and distorted (often harboring _TP53_ mutations). Anaplasia can be focal (confined to one area) or diffuse (widespread), requiring more intensive multi-agent chemotherapy regimens.

## Staging & Treatment Pathways

Canadian pediatric oncology centers utilize protocols informed by the **Children's Oncology Group (COG)** and the **International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP)**:

| Stage         | Clinical Description                                                                                                                      | Typical Treatment Approach                                                                                                                                                             |
| :------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Stage I**   | Tumor is limited to the kidney and was completely removed surgically without rupture.                                                     | Surgery (radical nephroureterectomy) + 18 weeks of gentle dual chemotherapy (Vincristine + Dactinomycin). Radiation is usually avoided.                                                |
| **Stage II**  | Tumor extended beyond kidney into local fat or vessels but was completely resected.                                                       | Surgery + dual chemotherapy (Vincristine + Dactinomycin). Radiation generally avoided for favorable histology.                                                                         |
| **Stage III** | Residual non-hematogenous tumor confined to abdomen (e.g., positive surgical margins, tumor spillage, or involved abdominal lymph nodes). | Surgery + three-drug chemotherapy (Vincristine + Dactinomycin + Doxorubicin) + targeted flank/abdominal radiation therapy.                                                             |
| **Stage IV**  | Hematogenous metastases to distant sites (most commonly lungs, liver, or bone).                                                           | Multi-agent chemotherapy + surgical resection of primary tumor + whole-lung radiation (if pulmonary nodules persist).                                                                  |
| **Stage V**   | Bilateral renal involvement (both kidneys affected at diagnosis).                                                                         | **Renal-sparing approach:** Preoperative chemotherapy to shrink tumors in both kidneys first, followed by nephron-sparing partial nephrectomies to preserve long-term kidney function. |

---

## What to Expect During Surgery & Beyond

- **Surgical Expertise:** Surgery is performed by specialized pediatric general surgeons at Canadian quaternary centers (e.g., SickKids, CHU Sainte-Justine, BC Children's, McMaster Children's, Stollery, IWK Health Centre).
- **Living with One Kidney:** Children who undergo complete removal of one kidney (nephrectomy) lead full, active, and athletic lives. The remaining single kidney enlarges (compensatory hypertrophy) and provides normal lifetime renal filtration.
- **Long-Term Monitoring:** Routine blood pressure monitoring, urine dipsticks, and renal ultrasounds ensure the remaining kidney stays completely healthy into adulthood.
