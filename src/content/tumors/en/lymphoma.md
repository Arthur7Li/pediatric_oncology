Receiving a diagnosis of lymphoma for your child is incredibly overwhelming. Lymphoma is a cancer of the lymphatic system, which is a crucial part of the body's immune system. Fortunately, pediatric lymphomas are highly treatable, and thanks to collaborative research across Canadian and international centers, survival rates are excellent.

This guide explains the different types of pediatric lymphoma, how they are diagnosed and staged, and the standard treatment protocols used in Canada, all in compassionate and accessible terms.

---

## What is Lymphoma?

The lymphatic system is made up of a network of vessels and small, bean-shaped organs called lymph nodes that carry a clear fluid called lymph. Lymph contains white blood cells (lymphocytes) that help fight infection.

Lymphoma occurs when these lymphocytes change and grow out of control. Because lymphatic tissue is found throughout the body—in lymph nodes, the spleen, the thymus, the bone marrow, and other organs—lymphoma can start almost anywhere.

There are two main categories of lymphoma in children: **Hodgkin Lymphoma** and **Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma**.

---

### Hodgkin Lymphoma (HL)

Hodgkin Lymphoma is characterized by the presence of a specific type of abnormal cell called the Reed-Sternberg cell. It is most common in adolescents and young adults (ages 15-39).

- **Symptoms:** The most common sign is a painless, swollen lymph node, usually in the neck, above the collarbone, or under the arm.
- **"B Symptoms":** About a third of patients experience systemic symptoms known as "B symptoms." These include:
  - Unexplained fever (above 38.0°C / 100.4°F)
  - Drenching night sweats
  - Significant, unexplained weight loss (more than 10% of body weight over 6 months)

### Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL)

Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma is a diverse group of blood cancers that do not contain Reed-Sternberg cells. In children, NHL tends to grow very quickly and requires immediate treatment. It is more common in younger children than HL.

Major subtypes of pediatric NHL include:

1. **Burkitt Lymphoma:** Often starts in the abdomen (causing pain, swelling, or bowel obstruction) or the tonsils/neck. It is one of the fastest-growing tumors in humans but is highly curable.
2. **Lymphoblastic Lymphoma:** Very similar to Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL), often presenting as a mass in the chest (mediastinum) which can cause breathing difficulties.
3. **Large Cell Lymphomas:** Including Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) and Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma (ALCL), which can start in lymph nodes or other organs like the skin or bone.

## Diagnosis and Staging

If lymphoma is suspected, your child's medical team (at centers like SickKids, CHU Sainte-Justine, or BC Children's) will perform a series of tests to determine the exact type and extent of the disease.

- **Biopsy:** The only way to definitively diagnose lymphoma is to remove a lymph node (or part of it) and examine it under a microscope.
- **Imaging Scans:** PET (Positron Emission Tomography) and CT (Computed Tomography) scans are used to see where the lymphoma has spread in the body.
- **Bone Marrow Aspiration/Biopsy and Lumbar Puncture:** Often performed to see if the cancer has spread to the bone marrow or the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

### Staging

Staging describes how far the cancer has spread. Pediatric lymphomas often use a 4-stage system (like the Ann Arbor staging system for HL or St. Jude staging for NHL):

- **Stage I & II:** Localized disease, involving one or two areas of lymph nodes on the same side of the diaphragm (the breathing muscle under the lungs).
- **Stage III:** Advanced disease, involving lymph nodes on both sides of the diaphragm, or disease that started in the abdomen.
- **Stage IV:** Widespread disease that has spread beyond the lymph nodes to organs like the bone marrow, liver, or central nervous system.

## The Treatment Roadmap in Canada

Canadian pediatric oncology centers follow highly standardized, evidence-based protocols, often developed in collaboration with the **Children's Oncology Group (COG)** and the **C17 Council**.

### Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is the primary treatment for almost all pediatric lymphomas. The specific drugs, doses, and duration depend on the type of lymphoma (HL vs. NHL) and the stage.

- **For HL:** Treatment typically involves cycles of multi-drug chemotherapy. To minimize long-term side effects, modern protocols aim to use the lowest effective dose of chemotherapy.
- **For NHL:** Treatment is generally intensive and rapid, especially for fast-growing types like Burkitt Lymphoma. It often involves high-dose chemotherapy given over a period of weeks to months.

### Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy uses high-energy rays to destroy cancer cells.

- **In HL:** It is sometimes used for specific, localized areas of disease, particularly if the initial tumor was very large (bulky disease) or didn't fully respond to chemotherapy.
- **In NHL:** Radiation is rarely used, as systemic chemotherapy is usually sufficient.
- _Note:_ Canadian centers are increasingly exploring **Proton Therapy** (often requiring travel to specialized facilities, sometimes out-of-country, coordinated by your provincial health plan) to minimize radiation exposure to healthy tissues, especially for tumors near the heart or lungs.

### Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

Recent advances have introduced new treatments that specifically target cancer cells, often with fewer side effects than traditional chemotherapy:

- **Brentuximab vedotin:** An antibody-drug conjugate used for certain types of HL and ALCL.
- **Rituximab:** A monoclonal antibody that targets the CD20 protein on B-cells, commonly added to chemotherapy for mature B-cell NHL (like Burkitt Lymphoma).
- **CAR T-cell Therapy:** For lymphomas that relapse or do not respond to initial treatment, this innovative therapy (reprogramming the child's own immune cells) is becoming available at major Canadian centers.

## Supporting Your Child at Home

- **Infection Prevention:** Chemotherapy severely weakens the immune system. Practice strict hand hygiene and contact your oncology team immediately for any fever.
- **Nutrition:** Nausea and mouth sores can make eating difficult. Work with your hospital's dietitian to find appealing, nutrient-dense foods.
- **Emotional Support:** A cancer diagnosis affects the whole family. Utilize the Child Life Specialists, social workers, and psychological support services available at your pediatric center.
