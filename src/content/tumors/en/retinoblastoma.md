Hearing that your child has cancer in their eye is a uniquely frightening experience. Retinoblastoma is a rare childhood cancer that starts in the retina, the light-sensitive lining at the back of the eye. While the diagnosis is daunting, retinoblastoma is highly curable when caught early.

Today, treatment focuses not only on saving the child's life but also on preserving the eye and maximizing vision. Canada is a global leader in retinoblastoma research and treatment, offering world-class care to affected families.

This guide provides a compassionate overview of retinoblastoma, its genetic links, and the advanced treatments available in Canada.

---

## What is Retinoblastoma?

The retina is made of nerve tissue that senses light and sends images to the brain. During the early stages of a baby's development, the eyes have cells called retinoblasts that divide into new cells and fill the retina. At a certain point, these cells stop dividing and develop into mature retinal cells.

In retinoblastoma, a genetic mutation causes these cells to continue dividing uncontrollably, forming a tumor.

### Unilateral vs. Bilateral Disease

Retinoblastoma can present in two ways, which strongly relates to its genetic cause:

- **Unilateral Retinoblastoma (One Eye):** Accounts for about 60-75% of cases. The tumor is present in only one eye. Most unilateral cases are non-hereditary (sporadic), meaning the genetic mutation happened by chance in a single cell in the eye.
- **Bilateral Retinoblastoma (Both Eyes):** Accounts for about 25-40% of cases. Tumors develop in both eyes. Bilateral retinoblastoma is almost always hereditary.

### The Genetic Link (The _RB1_ Gene)

Retinoblastoma is caused by a mutation in the _RB1_ gene, which normally acts as a tumor suppressor.

- In **hereditary retinoblastoma**, the child inherits one mutated copy of the _RB1_ gene from a parent (or the mutation occurs very early in embryonic development), and the mutation is present in every cell of their body. A second mutation then occurs in the retinal cells, triggering the cancer. Children with the hereditary form have a higher risk of developing other types of cancers later in life.
- In **non-hereditary retinoblastoma**, both _RB1_ mutations occur sporadically only in the retinal cells.

**Genetic Counseling:** If your child is diagnosed with retinoblastoma, a referral to a clinical geneticist is a critical part of the Canadian standard of care. Genetic testing can determine if the mutation is hereditary, which helps guide treatment, long-term screening, and family planning, as siblings may also need to be screened.

## Common Symptoms

Because retinoblastoma usually affects infants and toddlers who cannot communicate vision problems, parents or pediatricians are often the first to notice signs:

- **Leukocoria (White Pupil):** The most common sign. Instead of the normal "red eye" reflection seen in flash photographs, the pupil may appear white or have a white glow (sometimes called a "cat's eye" reflex).
- **Strabismus (Crossed or Wandering Eye):** The eyes may not look in the same direction. While common in infants, strabismus that persists or develops suddenly should be evaluated.
- **Other Signs:** Redness, swelling, or persistent pain in the eye, or a change in the color of the iris.

## Diagnosis and Staging

If retinoblastoma is suspected, your child will be referred to a specialized ocular oncologist (an eye cancer specialist).

- **Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA):** To thoroughly examine the retina and accurately stage the tumor, the doctor will use specialized lights and lenses while your child is safely asleep under general anesthesia.
- **Imaging:** Ultrasound of the eye and an MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) of the brain and orbits are used to see the exact size of the tumor and ensure it has not spread outside the eye or to the brain.

Staging for retinoblastoma is complex (often using the International Classification of Retinoblastoma) and groups tumors from Group A (small, confined tumors) to Group E (extensive tumors where the eye may not be savable).

## The Treatment Roadmap in Canada

The primary goal of treatment is to cure the cancer and save the child's life. The secondary, but vital, goals are to save the eye and preserve as much vision as possible.

Treatment is highly individualized and managed by specialized multidisciplinary teams. **The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) in Toronto** is Canada's National Retinoblastoma Referral Centre, and patients from across the country often travel there for specialized care, supported by provincial health plans.

### 1. Focal Therapies (For smaller tumors)

- **Laser Therapy (Transpupillary Thermotherapy - TTT):** Uses an infrared laser to heat and destroy cancer cells.
- **Cryotherapy:** Uses extreme cold to freeze and destroy the tumor.
  These are often performed during an EUA.

### 2. Chemotherapy

- **Systemic Chemotherapy:** Given intravenously (IV), circulating throughout the body to shrink tumors in the eye (chemoreduction), often making them small enough to be treated with focal therapies.
- **Intra-arterial Chemotherapy (IAC):** A highly specialized, targeted treatment where chemotherapy is delivered directly into the ophthalmic artery (the blood vessel supplying the eye) via a tiny catheter threaded from the groin. This delivers a high dose of medication to the tumor while minimizing systemic side effects. SickKids is a leading center for IAC in Canada.
- **Intravitreal Chemotherapy:** Chemotherapy injected directly into the jelly-like substance inside the eye (the vitreous), used when cancer cells are floating freely in the eye.

### 3. Surgery (Enucleation)

For advanced retinoblastoma (Group E) where the tumor has destroyed vision or poses a risk of spreading outside the eye, the safest and most curative option is surgical removal of the eye (enucleation).

- While emotionally devastating for parents, children adapt remarkably well to having one eye.
- Following surgery, an ocularist will create a custom-matched artificial eye (prosthesis) that looks very natural and moves with the eye muscles.

---

## Supporting Your Child

- **Protecting the Healthy Eye:** For children with unilateral retinoblastoma, protecting their good eye is critical. Impact-resistant polycarbonate glasses (even without a prescription) should be worn during play and sports.
- **Follow-up Care:** Frequent EUAs are required for years after treatment to ensure the cancer has not returned and to monitor for new tumors, especially in children with the hereditary form.
- **Emotional Support:** The journey involves frequent hospital visits and anxiety. Connect with social workers and parent support groups to help navigate this challenging time.
