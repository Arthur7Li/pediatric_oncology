A diagnosis of neuroblastoma can feel dizzying and terrifying. Neuroblastoma is a complex cancer of the developing nervous system that behaves very differently depending on a child's age, the tumor's genetic profile, and its stage. In some infants, tumors may mature or resolve on their own; in older children, aggressive multimodal therapy is required.

This guide provides clear, empathetic, and medically accurate information on neuroblastoma, Canadian treatment standards, and what families can expect.

---

## What is Neuroblastoma?

Neuroblastoma develops from **neuroblasts**—immature nerve cells of the sympathetic nervous system that are part of early embryonic development. Instead of maturing into functioning nerve tissue, these cells continue multiplying into a solid tumor.

Because the sympathetic nervous system runs from the base of the skull down along the spine into the pelvis, neuroblastoma can begin anywhere along this chain:

- **Adrenal Gland (Abdomen):** Approximately 65% of neuroblastomas start in the adrenal glands (small triangle-shaped glands sitting atop each kidney).
- **Paraspinal Ganglia:** Around 15–20% develop in the chest or neck along the spine.
- **Pelvis:** Around 5% start in the pelvic sympathetic chain.

## Key Genetic & Molecular Markers

At major Canadian cancer centers, the tumor tissue is analyzed for critical molecular features that dictate therapy:

- **MYCN Gene Amplification:** _MYCN_ is an oncogene that regulates cell growth. If the tumor has many extra copies of _MYCN_ (amplified), it indicates a faster-growing disease that requires intensive treatment, regardless of the child's age.
- **Chromosomal Changes:** Segmental chromosome alterations (such as 1p deletion, 11q deletion, or 17q gain) provide crucial prognostic information.
- **ALK Gene Mutations:** Present in roughly 10–14% of neuroblastomas, allowing oncologists to potentially use targeted ALK inhibitor medications (e.g., lorlatinib) in clinical trials.

## Common Signs and Symptoms

Because neuroblastoma can arise in different body regions, symptoms vary widely:

- **Abdominal Swelling or Firm Lump:** Often discovered during a routine bath or physical exam; can cause fullness or constipation.
- **Periorbital Ecchymosis ("Raccoon Eyes"):** Dark bruising around both eyes caused by metastatic cells settling in the thin bones of the eye sockets.
- **Bone Pain or Limping:** Pain in the legs or back when cancer cells involve bone or bone marrow.
- **Opsoclonus-Myoclonus-Ataxia Syndrome (OMS):** A rare paraneoplastic autoimmune reaction causing rapid involuntary eye movements ("dancing eyes") and jerking muscle spasms ("dancing feet").
- **Horner's Syndrome:** Drooping eyelid, small pupil, and lack of sweating on one side of the face if the tumor is located in the neck or upper chest.

## Staging & Risk Stratification (INRGSS)

Modern treatment is guided by the **International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS)**, which assesses Image-Defined Risk Factors (IDRFs) before surgery:

| Stage            | Definition                                                                                                                                                                                |
| :--------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **L1**           | Localized tumor that has not spread and does not involve vital blood vessels or organs (no IDRFs).                                                                                        |
| **L2**           | Localized tumor with one or more Image-Defined Risk Factors (wrapping around critical nerves or arteries).                                                                                |
| **M**            | Metastatic disease that has spread to distant lymph nodes, bone marrow, bone, or other organs.                                                                                            |
| **MS (Special)** | Metastatic disease in infants under 18 months confined only to the liver, skin, or minimal bone marrow, which frequently demonstrates spontaneous regression with supportive observation. |

## Treatment Strategies

Treatment is divided into three distinct risk tiers:

### 1. Low-Risk Neuroblastoma

- **Approach:** Surgical removal alone or careful active surveillance with ultrasound/MRI (especially in infants with small adrenal masses or MS disease).
- **Prognosis:** Long-term survival exceeds 95% with minimal intervention.

### 2. Intermediate-Risk Neuroblastoma

- **Approach:** 4 to 8 cycles of moderate chemotherapy (such as carboplatin, etoposide, cyclophosphamide, and doxorubicin) followed by surgical resection.
- **Prognosis:** Over 90% cure rate while avoiding toxic high-dose therapies.

### 3. High-Risk Neuroblastoma

- For children with metastatic disease (Stage M over 18 months) or localized tumors with _MYCN_ amplification. High-risk neuroblastoma requires an intensive 5-step multimodal strategy:
  1. **Induction Chemotherapy:** Rapid cycles of intensive multi-agent chemotherapy to shrink the tumor and clear bone marrow metastases.
  2. **Maximal Safe Surgery:** Neurosurgeons and pediatric surgeons resect the primary tumor.
  3. **Consolidation with Stem Cell Transplant:** High-dose chemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell rescue (sometimes tandem transplants) to destroy microscopic residual cancer cells.
  4. **Radiation Therapy:** Targeted external beam radiation to the primary tumor site and persistent metastatic areas.
  5. **Post-Consolidation Immunotherapy:** Anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody therapy (**Dinutuximab**), administered alongside cytokines (GM-CSF) and isotretinoin (13-cis-retinoic acid) to train the child's immune system to eliminate any remaining single tumor cells.

## Navigating Care in Canada

Specialized care for high-risk neuroblastoma is coordinated through Canada's **C17 Council**, ensuring children across all provinces have access to unified COG clinical trials, modern immunotherapy, and specialized surgical expertise. Families facing travel from remote areas can access provincial travel grants and specialized support centers (such as Ronald McDonald Houses near SickKids, BC Children's, Stollery, and CHU Sainte-Justine).
